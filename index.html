The Symmetries of the
Alhambra

On a hilltop, against the backdrop of the Sierra Nevada mountains in Granada, Spain, lies the Alhambra fortress. This palace is a relic of Islamic rule in Iberia, which lasted nearly eight centuries. It stands as the crowning achievement of the Nasrid dynasty, and a witness to its fall.

As imposing as the Alhambra is from the outside, as soon as you step in, in the words of American writer Washington Irving, you find "poetry breathing throughout the fairy architecture of its halls." Within, finely carved arabesques, calligraphic verses, and wonderfully symmetric patterns adorn the walls. The palace's artwork has awed its travelers and captivated the minds of many, including one obsessive Dutch printmaker.

...And mathematicians are no exception...

The Alhambra is a treasure trove of what mathematicians call wallpaper patterns. These are designs that, in principle, continue forever, and are built from a motif repeated along two independent directions. What makes them interesting and beautiful are their symmetries!

If a group theorist walked into the Alhambra, she might be compelled to start sorting its patterns, placing two patterns in the same category when they have the same underlying symmetries. After sorting for a while, though, a question would naturally arise: just how many categories are there?

On first considering this question, one might intuitively answer: "Surely a huge number of categories exist. Perhaps even infinitely many. After all, artists can get as creative as they like cooking up these patterns." But the answer is... 17: every wallpaper pattern falls into one of just 17 categories, known formally as the 17 wallpaper groups.

And here is what's special about the Alhambra: it is claimed to be the only place in the world to exhibit examples of all 17 groups.

On this page, we discuss whether the Alhambra really has all 17 groups, and we explain why it is that only 17 exist, and not 16 or infinitely many. To do so, we do not take the traditional algebraic route (which is how a group theorist might solve this). Instead, we present the ideas from the book “The Symmetries of Things” by mathematicians John Conway, Heidi Burgiel, and Chaim Goodman-Strauss, treating these patterns not as flat walls, but as folded-up topological shapes called orbifolds.

The content is divided into sections. We start by discussing the symmetries of the Euclidean plane. We follow up by learning about a notation used to describe the symmetries of wallpaper patterns. After, we learn about how this notation really describes topological orbifolds. We take a detour to learn about surfaces and Euler's Characteristic, and connect it all together when we discuss the Magic Theorem which proves the 17 wallpaper groups. We present examples from the Alhambra of the 17 group types. We wrap it all up in the conclusion and discuss other curiosities of the Alhambra.
